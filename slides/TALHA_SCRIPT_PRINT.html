2The problem, and the four gaps0:58

Let me start with what a traffic classifier actually sees.

Not the whole flow. Just the first B bytes, zero-padded to a fixed length. That prefix is what we call the observation budget.

And it is not a minor setting. It decides three things: how quickly you can detect, how much memory you need per flow, and how early you can act on the answer.

So we checked what the field does. Twelve papers, 2025 and 2026. Budgets from five to twenty packets, or 784 to 1024 bytes. Not one of them measured it.

That is the first of four gaps. The budget is inherited rather than measured. The aggregate hides per-class failure. Corpora are called encrypted without anyone checking. And shortcut learning is rarely excluded, which matters: one of our own base papers shows that leaving identifier bytes in is worth up to 0.45 accuracy.

3The padding confound, and the objectives1:04

Now the part that makes this more than an unexamined setting.

Flows are short. The median ISCX session is 274 bytes. USTC is 474.

So if your budget is 1024, you are not reading more traffic. You are reading more padding. At 512 bytes almost half of an average ISCX session is padding. At 1500 it is seventy percent.

Which means budget and padding always move together. Any gain someone reports from a bigger budget mixes two things: more real bytes, and more padding the model can use as a length cue. Nobody separates them. So a paper saying “1024 bytes works best” may have measured a length classifier.

That gives us five objectives, on the right. Measure the curve and find where it saturates. Test whether that is the model or the traffic. Split padding into its two parts. Build a classifier that reads only what it needs. And report honestly, including what the train-test split alone is worth.

4The pipeline and the leakage gate0:51

So, how we measured it.

One requirement drove the whole design. If the budget is the thing being measured, it has to be the only thing that changes.

So a single pass writes a fixed 2048-byte store, and every budget is a slice of that one store. Parsing, sessionization, deduplication and anonymisation all happen once, before any budget exists. Nothing can drift between 256 bytes and 512.

On the right is the leakage gate. Gap four is usually handled with one sentence promising identifiers were removed. We made it a condition the pipeline enforces.

A probe trains on the anonymised identifier bytes alone. If it beats guessing by more than a small margin, the anonymisation has failed and the run stops. It does not warn. It stops.

5Padding decomposed, and the classifier1:06

Two pieces of method here.

On the left, five input modes. The literature changes the representation and the padding symbol together, then credits the whole gain to padding-aware input. We change one at a time. Zero to embed gives the representation term. Embed to mask gives the padding-symbol term.

On the right, the classifier. Heads at 64, 128, 256, 512 and 1024 bytes. At inference you stop at the first head that is confident enough, and charge the flow only the bytes that head used.

But that only works if head k genuinely cannot see byte k plus one.

So the encoder is causal. Six dilated convolution blocks, left-padding only, receptive field 505 bytes. And the normalisation is per-position, never BatchNorm or GroupNorm, because both average across the length axis. One wrong line there and a byte at position 900 could shift the activation at position 10.

We prove that by induction. We also test it, and I will show you the test in a moment.
